**Reviewed October 23, 2026.** Google Play treats device location as personal and sensitive data. Background access is limited to use cases that deliver a significant user benefit and are directly relevant to core functionality. It requires a Play Console permissions declaration and review. Android runtime permission and Google Play policy approval are separate layers: a system grant does not establish Play policy approval.

## Decide whether background access is necessary

- The core feature must be prominent in the app and store listing. Without it, the app's main purpose should be impaired or unusable. Background access must provide clear, significant user value and match reasonable user expectations.
- Prefer a narrower alternative when foreground, approximate location, or the Android Location Button can support the feature. Nearby-friend suggestions only while the app is open, local-content personalization, regional content restrictions, user-side delivery/service status, and foreground-only navigation may often work with foreground location. These examples do not automatically mean an app will be rejected; if they are the only reasons for background access, redesign rather than requesting broad access.
- Location may not be requested solely for advertising or analytics. Stop access when it is no longer needed for current functionality. Do not sell location data; the app is responsible for location use by third-party SDKs as well.
- Apps directed at children must comply with Families requirements; child-targeted apps may not request device-location permissions. Mixed-audience apps must also control data access and SDK behavior for children and unknown-age users.

Continuous family location sharing or a safety alert when a child crosses a geofence may provide a more direct safety benefit, but each app must still establish core-functionality need, user expectation, minimum scope, and clear disclosure. Examples are not pre-approved use cases.

### How Play weighs user benefit

The article lists physical safety, perceived safety, and health/fitness as significant user benefits. Ads or offers, analytics, personalization, entertainment, and convenience are examples of minimal benefits. Review also considers whether the feature is core to the app's purpose, whether users expect background access, whether foreground location can deliver the same experience, and whether the privacy policy appears in Console and in the app. The following foreground-capable examples do not automatically fail an app; if they are the only justification for background access, they are a reason to redesign the scope:

- nearby-friend/player suggestions only while the user is in the app;
- local-content personalization only while the app is open, without a closed-app alert or feature;
- regional digital-rights restrictions;
- customer-side delivery/service tracking (not driver tracking);
- turn-by-turn navigation conducted in the app, excluding passive route/step tracking or detecting when driving starts/stops after leaving the app; and
- aggregating location to show traffic congestion or nearby network speeds.

Google Play evaluates each app against its declared core functionality; appearing in an example is neither automatic approval nor automatic rejection.

## Play Console declaration and review materials

Complete the declaration in Play Console under **App content → Sensitive app permissions → Location permissions**. Complete any prerequisite App access or Authority declaration forms that Console requests. Select **one** location feature that best represents the app's core purpose, explain why it needs background location and why a narrower alternative cannot meet the need, and describe the user benefit. Declaring multiple background-location features may lead to rejection. Approval is a review of the app's permission use, not only that single feature.

Prepare at least:

1. **Permissions declaration:** explain the app's main purpose, the single selected background-location feature, necessity, and why alternatives are insufficient.
2. **Demonstration video:** aim for 30 seconds or less. Show the feature in the Android app, its background effect, the prominent disclosure, and the following system runtime prompt. If there is no visible UI while it runs, demonstrate its impact or notification. Do not submit iOS behavior as a substitute for Android behavior.
3. **Prominent in-app disclosure:** show it in the normal user flow before the location runtime prompt. It cannot live only in a privacy policy, terms, or settings menu, and should not be bundled with unrelated disclosures. Play's article says a separate “I agree” button is not required for this disclosure; the following system permission prompt handles consent.
4. **Privacy policy and store disclosure:** provide a privacy policy both in the app and on its store listing. Use an active, non-PDF, non-editable URL. Clearly identify it as the app/developer's privacy policy and describe location access, collection, use, recipients, and purposes.
5. **All-track review:** review applies to all active app bundles/APKs, including production, open testing, and closed testing. If background access is no longer justified, remove the permission and related code from every version and track, not only production.

## Required prominent-disclosure content

Clearly explain background-location use in the app and user-facing store materials. The in-app disclosure must appear directly in the normal usage flow, explain what location data is accessed/collected and how it is used/shared, and:

- include the word “location”;
- state that location is accessed in the background, while the app is closed, or when it is not in use;
- list every feature that uses background location; and
- if the actual use includes ads, accurately say that it supports advertising and follow the separate location-for-ads policy.

A useful structure is: “This app collects location data to enable [specific feature] even when the app is closed or not in use.” Treat this as a writing pattern, not a compliance guarantee; align it with actual feature and SDK behavior.

Android's audit checklist starts with the manifest and location APIs, then checks background services, job schedulers, alarms, component PendingIntents, and SDKs. Google Play also states that every active track is reviewable.

### AlarmManager and background location are separate permission questions

`AlarmManager` schedules when an operation runs. Exact-alarm access does not grant location access or create a Google Play background-location eligibility or exemption. If an alarm triggers a receiver or service while the app is not running, that component still needs the applicable Android location authorization and must satisfy background-location restrictions and Play policy. Android has an exception to foreground-service background-start limits for an exact alarm completing a user-requested action; this exception concerns starting the foreground service and does not replace location permission or background-location review. On Android 14 and higher, a location foreground service that needs a while-in-use location permission is still subject to separate service-creation restrictions when the app is in the background, unless another exception for that permission applies.

Choose `SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM` or `USE_EXACT_ALARM` according to Android version and target SDK. Their Android grant behavior and Play eligibility/declaration requirements are summarized in [Sensitive permissions and APIs](../sensitive-permissions/sensitive-permissions.en-US.md). Triggering a location task with an alarm, WorkManager, or JobScheduler does not itself make background location eligible.

## Foreground services are related but distinct

A location foreground service must continue an in-app, user-initiated action and stop as soon as that requested task is complete. For Android 14+, each foreground service needs a valid declared type and matching permission, with Console declarations as required. If location through an FGS is functionally equivalent to background location, the background-location core-function, disclosure, and review requirements still apply; the permission name or service type is not a bypass. Play will remove geofencing as an approved FGS use case on January 27, 2027; see the foreground services and geofencing topic ([Foreground services and geofencing migration](../foreground-services-and-geofencing/foreground-services-and-geofencing.en-US.md)) for migration, best practices, and boundaries around the Android Geofence API.

Android 17/API 37 minimum-scope requirements for foreground precise location are covered separately in Contacts and foreground location ([Contacts and foreground location: minimum scope and alternatives](../contacts-location-permissions/contacts-location-permissions.en-US.md)): use the Location Button for session-only precise location; assess background location under this page's stricter criteria. Check the [Policy Deadlines](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/table/12921780?hl=en) page for the January 27, 2027 geofencing-related foreground-service transition to the Geofence API.

## Pre-release checklist

1. Search every feature, manifest, source tree, merged manifest, and SDK dependency for background location; record trigger, accuracy, frequency, recipients, and affected tracks.
2. Keep background access only when it is demonstrably essential to core functionality and significantly benefits users. Remove broad access when foreground, approximate location, or Location Button can serve the use case.
3. If applying, declare one core location feature in Console and align the declaration, in-app flow, store listing, privacy policy, video, and SDK behavior.
4. Show prominent disclosure before the runtime prompt; retain reasonable functionality after denial and guide users to Settings accurately without misleading them.
5. Check production and testing tracks, third-party SDKs, child/mixed-audience implications, and retain declaration and review-change records.
