## Prominent disclosure and consent: turn policy into reviewable UI

Google Play's best-practices guide recommends:

- Show the disclosure in the app, close to the permission or capability request. A store listing or website alone is insufficient.
- Offer clear choices to consent or decline/defer. After refusal or revocation, keep unrelated parts of the app usable where possible, and respect repeated refusals.
- Do not imitate Android system permission prompts. Use friendly, clear language that a 13-year-old can understand; clarity can matter more than brevity.
- Structure the explanation around Why / What / How: why the capability is needed, what data is involved, and how it supports a core feature. If an SDK collects or shares data with a third party, identify the data, purpose, and recipient.
- If a Play Console permission declaration requires a video, show app launch, the path to disclosure, the full text (scroll slowly to the end), consent and refusal/re-entry flows, and a core feature using the capability. Add captions or narration if the feature's use is unclear.

This is a **best-practices guide**, not a replacement for binding policy. It also does not replace the privacy policy, Data safety declaration, Android runtime permission, or legally required consent; these are separate controls and should agree. The guide links to the Play Academy course [Declared permissions and in-app disclosures](https://playacademy.exceedlms.com/student/activity/66910), whose visible course outline is available but whose lesson/video body could not be read publicly. The Play Console declaration form requires account access.

## CNIL mobile-app recommendations: additional French/EU privacy context

### Current version and scope

The current authoritative French text is the CNIL's [98-page modified recommendations PDF published April 8, 2025](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/cnil/files/2025-04/recommandation-applications-mobiles-modifiee.pdf). It was adopted through Decision No. 2025-024 of March 27, 2025: Article 1 repeals the 2024-061 decision and Article 2 adopts the modified recommendation. The accessible [CNIL revision notice](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/recommandations-applications-mobiles-modifiee) lists the French PDF, a [100-page annotated revision](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/default/files/2025-04/recommandation-applications-mobiles-modifiee_suivi.pdf), and the Legifrance decision. CNIL describes the changes as corrections, omissions, and clarifications rather than a substantive overhaul. The official decision record's search-index excerpt identifies three key edits: correcting a role example for location data handled by an SDK for a publisher; replacing the old CMP/OS-permission sequence table with an infographic; and clarifying that an SDK's own-purpose processing must be contractually documented at integration. The exact Legifrance URL still returns 403, so this detail is attributed to the CNIL notice and official search-index excerpt, not a full direct read of the Legifrance page.

The 93-page [English courtesy translation](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/cnil/files/2025-05/recommendation-mobiles-app.pdf), linked from the original English User Data news page, covers the same 2025 revision but states that only the French version is legally binding. It helps identify roles and duties under laws such as GDPR/ePrivacy. It mixes legal-duty explanations with regulatory recommendations and good practices; the CNIL checklists explicitly say some checks are recommendations or good practices, not obligations. Google Play User Data points to CNIL as potentially helpful context; it does not incorporate the entire document into Play policy.

The [English CNIL news page](https://www.cnil.fr/en/mobile-applications-cnil-publishes-its-recommendations-better-privacy-protection) linked directly from User Data reopened in this review. ### Practical best practices: make SDK and permission reviews verifiable

Section 6.3 (approximately pp. 52–54) of the English courtesy translation can inform an engineering **privacy-review checklist**. Classify each item as legal duty, recommendation, or good practice as the source does:

1. Compare SDK docs, the merged manifest, and runtime behavior. Record permissions, purposes, data, recipients, and roles; separate core-function needs from convenience and verify the core still works when optional permission is refused.
2. Consider narrower permissions, lower precision, and on-device processing. Configure independent SDK purposes separately. Where feasible, integrate only function-level modules, avoiding unused components, permissions, and attack surface.
3. Test before/after consent, per-purpose accept/refuse, withdrawal, and SDK updates. CNIL says network-interception auditing may be considered to check that non-exempt reads/writes do not occur before consent; choices are respected; observed data matches the processing record; protected resources are used only by authorized functions; and withdrawal works.
4. Review SDK version changes. Re-run data-flow and authorization checks after updates, and request provider documentation on processing, permissions, and tracking technologies.

These prompts can support consistency checks across Google Play User Data, prominent disclosure, Data safety, permission declarations, and SDK Index. They are not a universal test protocol and do not replace Play Console forms or videos. Current Play policy remains the source for binding Play duties.

## Follow-up audit: technical permissions and geolocation

### OS permissions, legal consent, and Play disclosure are separate controls

The CNIL [permissions explainer](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/permissions-applications-mobiles-recommandations-de-la-cnil-pour-respecter-la-vie-privee), dated January 14, 2025, was directly readable in this review. It describes Android/iOS OS permissions as **technical access controls** over device resources such as the camera, location, and contacts. A permission prompt generally does not state the purpose of processing and does not by itself establish valid consent under the GDPR/French law. The app publisher must separately determine whether consent is legally required; in most cases where consent is required, a CMP must supplement the OS permission. Only limited cases—such as one permission for one processing operation, one purpose, and one recipient—may allow the permission prompt itself to carry consent.

CNIL recommends contextual consent requests rather than a single catch-all screen at launch. A CMP and OS permission can appear in either order if the sequence remains understandable. If one authorization is refused, avoid displaying the other unnecessary request. OS permission design should support granular precision, data scope, and authorization duration, and apps should choose the least intrusive permission that meets the purpose. The [one-page permissions/CMP flowchart](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/cnil/files/2025-01/infographie_permissions_et_cmp.pdf) illustrates both orders when the two authorizations are needed. These are CNIL's French/EU privacy guidance; Google's prominent-disclosure and affirmative-consent duties and Android runtime permissions must still be assessed separately.

The [CNIL OS definition](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/definition/systeme-dexploitation-ou-operating-system-os) describes an OS as managing device resources, app sandboxing, and the permission system. It is terminology, not an Android permission API guide or Play review clause.

### CNIL's July 2026 geolocation guidance

On July 7, 2026, CNIL published a [professional guide to location processing in mobile apps](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/geolocalisation-applications-mobiles-quelles-regles). Location is personal data when it can identify a person and is governed by the GDPR and French electronic-communications privacy rules. App teams should explain purposes, recipients, retention, and rights; where location is not strictly necessary to provide the requested service (for example, targeted advertising or sale to data brokers), CNIL says valid consent is required. Consent does not remove the duties to minimize precision, purposes, and retention. Prefer on-device processing when feasible, and do not collect continuously in the background when the feature does not need it. OS-level precise/approximate or foreground/background authorization is not itself legal consent.

CNIL's companion [public geolocation privacy guide](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/geolocalisation-applications-mobiles-proteger-vos-donnees), published the same day, advises users to prefer “while using the app” access, check data uses and third-party sharing, revoke unnecessary permissions, and delete location history. This is user guidance, not the sole basis for developer duties or Google Play rules.

Use these sources alongside Play's [User Data policy](../user-data/user-data.en-US.md), [Background Location topic](../background-location/background-location.en-US.md), and [Contacts/Location preview](../contacts-location-permissions/contacts-location-permissions.en-US.md): Play review, Android access controls, and the GDPR/ePrivacy legal basis are separate layers; satisfying one does not satisfy the others.

### Historical-link correction: Privacy Shield is no longer valid

Google Play's [June 2017 policy announcement](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14554580) said it was introducing EU–U.S. Privacy Shield data-protection guidance. The English route still returns HTTP 429; on October 24, 2026, the complete short body opened at the official [Simplified Chinese `zh-Hans` route](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14554580?hl=zh-Hans). It contains one historical paragraph and links to legacy User Data route `answer/9888076`, which the existing URL audit records as redirecting to current `answer/10144311`. On July 16, 2020, the EU Court of Justice invalidated Privacy Shield adequacy Decision 2016/1250 in Schrems II. On July 10, 2023, the European Commission adopted the EU–U.S. Data Privacy Framework (DPF) adequacy [Implementing Decision (EU) 2023/1795](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dec_impl/2023/1795/oj). As of October 23, 2026, the Commission's [adequacy-decision directory](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/international-dimension-data-protection/adequacy-decisions_en) still lists U.S. commercial organisations participating in the EU–U.S. DPF as covered recipients. In its first review in 2024, the Commission concluded that the necessary structures and procedures had been put in place, noted that practical experience was still limited, called for stronger compliance monitoring, and recommended the next periodic review after three years.

This timeline corrects the historical reference in the Play announcement; the DPF does not become a separate Google Play review rule. It also does not mean that every U.S. company, developer, or data flow is automatically covered. Verify the organisation's current certification and the scope of the data and service, while continuing to meet applicable law, contract, User Data, and Data safety requirements. Sources: the [Court of Justice press release](https://curia.europa.eu/site/upload/docs/application/pdf/2020-07/cp200091en.pdf) and the [European Commission's first DPF review report (2024)](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/25695177-8073-4ce3-bf81-eb816dc6b468_en?filename=Report+on+the+first+periodic+review+of+the+functioning+of+the+adequacy+decision+on+the+EU-US+Data+Privacy+Framework.pdf). | # | Seven core principles |
| ---: | --- |
| 1 | Notice: A clear, conspicuous privacy notice should cover participation, data types/purposes and covered entities, recipients and purposes, individual rights and choices, complaint and free redress routes, regulators, possible arbitration, lawful public-authority access, and onward-transfer responsibility. It must be available at collection or as soon as practicable, and before a materially different use or first third-party disclosure. |
| 2 | Choice: Individuals generally receive a usable opt-out for disclosure to a third-party controller or a materially different purpose. Sensitive information generally requires affirmative express consent for those uses. The choice exception for an agent acting only on the organization’s instructions still requires a contract. |
| 3 | Accountability for Onward Transfer: Apply recipient-role-specific limits, equivalent protection, oversight, and remediation. Agents must act only on instructions; the participating organization must take reasonable steps to confirm compliance and stop or correct unauthorized processing. |
| 4 | Security: Use reasonable and appropriate safeguards against loss, misuse, and unauthorized access, disclosure, alteration, or destruction, proportionate to risk and data type. |
| 5 | Data Integrity and Purpose Limitation: Keep only information relevant to the processing purpose; avoid incompatible use; maintain data quality as needed; and retain identifiable information only while the purpose requires it. Certain public-interest archival, journalistic, artistic, scientific/historical research, and statistical uses may continue longer, subject to the other principles. |
| 6 | Access: Individuals may confirm whether their data is processed, obtain it, and seek correction, amendment, or deletion of inaccurate or improperly processed data. Limits are narrow and include disproportionate burden or other people’s rights; separable non-confidential information should still be provided. |
| 7 | Recourse, Enforcement and Liability: Provide effective, free and accessible independent complaint routes, verify that public commitments are implemented, and impose meaningful remediation and sanctions. Eligible unresolved individual claims may proceed to binding arbitration; the participating organization generally remains responsible for an agent’s non-compliant processing. |

Annex I’s EU-U.S. arbitral model addresses scope, prior remedies, arbitration notice/eligibility, panel composition, procedure, and costs. ## Children's personal data under the GDPR: Article 8 scope, verification, and Play mapping

This branch follows the GDPR example cited by the Families program. It opens the European Commission's [specific safeguards for children's data](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/legal-grounds-processing-data/are-there-any-specific-safeguards-data-about-children_en) and its public-facing [Information for individuals](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-individuals_en) page, then follows the child-data subsection into the EDPB's [Guidelines 05/2020 on consent](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/documents/guideline/guidelines-052020-on-consent-under-regulation-2016679_en). These materials explain applicable law; they do not automatically create new Play review clauses.

### Article 8 does not mean parental consent is needed for every child-data operation

The Commission's public-facing explanation says parental/guardian consent applies to consent-based processing of a child's personal data until the age set by each Member State, between 13 and 16. Check the national threshold in each target market. The EDPB guidance clarifies that the special Article 8 rule applies only when **both** conditions are met: processing relates to an **information society service offered directly to a child**, and the processing is **based on GDPR consent**. The GDPR's other child-protection, transparency, and general-lawfulness duties still apply, but Article 8 is not a universal age gate for every child-data operation or every app.

- The default Article 8 age is 16. A Member State may lower it by law, but not below 13. For cross-border services, check the rules in the Member States the service targets; the EDPB warns that a controller cannot always rely only on the law of its main establishment.
- Whether a service is offered directly to children depends on its positioning, content, and marketing. A service clearly limited to people 18 or over may fall outside this trigger if the actual content and marketing do not contradict that representation. An online component may qualify as an information society service; physical delivery of goods does not itself do so.
- Information directed to children must be easy to access and written in clear, plain language they can understand. Recital 38 highlights marketing, profiling, and services offered directly to children, but the EDPB says child protection is not confined to marketing or profiling.

### Verify age and parental authorization in proportion to risk, technology, and data minimization

Section 7.1 of the EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 (selected sections of the 33-page PDF) calls for reasonable verification efforts proportionate to processing risk and warns against excessive personal-data collection to prove age or parental responsibility. Lower-risk cases may use lighter approaches such as year of birth/self-declaration or a parent email, but a birth year is not reliable in every case; higher-risk processing may warrant stronger evidence. A trusted third-party check may reduce data the service itself receives. The GDPR does not prescribe one verification technology, and parental responsibility is not necessarily held only by a biological parent.

Parental authorization does not permanently replace the child's control. Once the child reaches the applicable digital-consent age, they can confirm, modify, or withdraw consent previously given/authorized by a parent; the controller should tell them about that option. In the scope described by the guidance, preventive or counselling services offered directly to a child do not require prior parental authorization. GDPR consent for data processing and a minor's capacity to enter a contract are parallel but distinct issues.

The full EDPB [Guidelines 05/2020 PDF](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/files/file1/edpb_guidelines_202005_consent_en.pdf) also says controllers must select the lawful basis for each purpose before processing. If consent was chosen, a controller cannot retrospectively relabel processing as legitimate interest to repair invalid or withdrawn consent. The readable [three-page summary (April 2026)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-04/edpb-summary-consent_en.pdf) summarizes the four cumulative consent elements—freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous—and says withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent.

### Version boundary for legitimate-interest material

The Commission's legal-grounds page links to the EDPB [Guidelines 1/2024 on legitimate interest](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/documents/guideline/guidelines-12024-on-processing-of-personal-data-based-on-article-61f-gdpr_en). The EDPB page currently lists public-consultation material from 2024; the opened [version 1.0 PDF](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-10/edpb_guidelines_202401_legitimateinterest_en.pdf) is explicitly marked “adopted—version for public consultation,” not a final version. Its three cumulative questions are whether a specific, current, lawful interest exists; whether processing is necessary for it; and whether data-subject rights/interests override it, with prior assessment and documentation. The EDPB's [two-page summary](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-10/edpb_summary_202401_legitimateinterest_en.pdf) gives a concise checklist. This helps explain Article 6(1)(f); it does not change Article 8's trigger or provide a retrospective substitute for invalid consent.
