## Scope and developer responsibility

Apps and developer accounts must not infringe third-party intellectual-property rights or encourage/induce others to infringe them. Covered rights include trademarks, copyright, patents, trade secrets, and other proprietary rights. The rule applies to the app, its icon/name/description/screenshots and other listing assets, and content displayed, played, sold, or distributed in the app.

- Use content you own, content with a license that clearly covers the use, or material for which a genuine legal exception applies. Buying a copy, giving attribution, using it non-commercially, modifying it, or adding “no infringement intended” does not itself create permission.
- Copying or distributing protected content, or helping users obtain it without authorization, can violate policy even when the app does not host the content itself. Keep verifiable evidence for third-party brands, logos, images, audio, or video, including rights holder, work, permitted use, market, term, and channel.
- Google may ask a developer to prove it has the right to use disputed material. When a complaint arrives, verify the exact asset and scope; remove or replace material where the app lacks rights, and update the app and listing.
- Misleading users into believing an app is endorsed by a brand or organization may also implicate [Impersonation](../impersonation/impersonation.en-US.md) and [Misrepresentation](../misrepresentation/misrepresentation.en-US.md). A license to use an asset does not automatically grant endorsement.

## Complaints and response paths

Copyright, trademark, and counterfeit-goods complaints use different legal/product flows. Google processes clear, valid copyright notices under the applicable legal process. A trademark owner should first try to contact the developer and may use the trademark form if unresolved. Counterfeit-goods sale or promotion has a separate form. A submitted complaint is not a judicial determination of ownership.

For a valid copyright removal request, the notice is generally forwarded to the app publisher. A counter-notice is forwarded to the complainant; disputes may require direct resolution or court proceedings. Google does not adjudicate ownership between the parties.

## Advance Notice: pre-submission for limited scenarios

Google Play's Advance Notice intake only handles listed situations, such as written third-party IP permission, government-app authorization, a recognized healthcare organization, a Korean GRAC certificate, an accessibility-service rationale, a verified nonprofit, or law-enforcement/regulator documentation. It is not general pre-review, guaranteed approval, or a review fast track; unsupported requests may not receive a response.

Submissions require app/package and developer details plus PDF evidence. Documents should identify the app/package and developer; do not remove those identifiers. Do not submit identity documents or unrelated personal, financial, or phone data. Signatures, dates, and authorization scope should match the current app and release cycle. Google says it tries to respond within two business days, but it may take longer; publishing before a response may result in rejection of that app submission.

## Pre-release checks

- [ ] Verify the source and license for app code, SDKs, user content, store text/images, brand elements, and marketing assets.
- [ ] Keep the original permission, rights holder identity, app/package, use case, territory, term, modification, and sublicensing scope.
- [ ] Do not infer permission from attribution, free/non-commercial use, self-modification, or public availability online.
- [ ] Route copyright/trademark claims, counterfeit reports, and Play impersonation/misrepresentation reports to their respective processes.
- [ ] Submit Advance Notice only for a supported case, with complete evidence; assess release risk while awaiting a response.
- [ ] If materials imply brand, government, or healthcare-provider endorsement, also check Impersonation and Misrepresentation.

### Government-information apps: requirements, direct links, and descendants

Google Play's [government-information apps article](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9514050?hl=en) explains the Deceptive Behavior policy; the article says the complete policy takes precedence. False government affiliation and offering or facilitating government services without authorization may violate policy. Authorized developers should obtain written proof. If an app facilitates a government process such as voting or ID validation, Google may require the relevant government entity to submit proof directly, with government contact details that can be validated. Apps that are not government-affiliated but communicate government information should make sources easy to see in the app description and store listing, and clearly state that they do not represent a government or political entity. `.gov` and `.go.jp` are examples that help users verify sources, not the only acceptable domains.

The guide also requires a privacy policy that explains user-data handling and limits use to disclosed purposes, discoverable both in the app and on the store listing. Developers must complete the Government apps declaration in Play Console; this requirement has applied since January 31, 2023. Completing the declaration only makes an app eligible for further verification of official-endorsement visual treatment; it does not guarantee a badge or endorsement. Advance Notice covers only listed submission scenarios and does not replace the Console declaration or provide general pre-review/expedited review.
