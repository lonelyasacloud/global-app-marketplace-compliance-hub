## Determine whether an app or data is in scope

An app is in scope if it offers health-related features or information, or accesses health data to support a non-health feature. Calling a feature “fitness,” “lifestyle,” or “entertainment,” or delegating collection to an SDK, does not by itself remove it from scope. Health and Health Connect data are also [personal and sensitive user data](../user-data/user-data.en-US.md); Data safety, privacy-policy, runtime permission, prominent disclosure, and affirmative-consent requirements continue to apply.

The Play Console health declaration categories include:

| Category | Typical features |
| --- | --- |
| Health and fitness | Activity/fitness, nutrition/weight management, period tracking, sleep, stress/relaxation/mental acuity |
| Medical | Disease management, clinical decision support, disease prevention/public health, emergency/first aid, healthcare services/management, mental/behavioral health, medical reference/education, medication/treatment, physical therapy/rehabilitation, reproductive/sexual health |
| Medical device apps | Software regulated for medical purposes, such as diagnosis, treatment recommendations, clinical monitoring, or integration with medical hardware |
| Human-subjects research | Health research, clinical trials, patient communities, and related study features |

Even apps with no health features must submit the declaration and certify that they have none. The requirement covers apps published on Google Play, including closed-testing, open-testing, and production tracks, and preloaded/pregranted apps updated through Play. System services and private apps are exempt. Update the declaration when functionality changes.

## Play Console, account, and store-listing requirements

- Complete the **Policy → App content → Health Apps** declaration in Play Console. Identify each health feature and explain how each permission or Health Connect data type directly supports a specific user-facing feature. “Needed by the app” is not a sufficient justification.
- Health, medical, and human-subjects research apps should use an **Organization developer account**. Organization account registration generally requires a D‑U‑N‑S number. See [Choose a developer account type](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13634885?hl=en).
- Provide a privacy-policy link in the Play Console field and a link or text in the app. It must be active, public, non-geofenced, and not a PDF. Explain how the app and third-party code access, collect, use, store, share, secure, and delete health data. The store-listing policy must match the one users see from Health Connect.
- Do not request health permissions that are not necessary for core functionality; remove unused permissions. Product behavior, store listing, permission declarations, Data safety, privacy policy, and in-app disclosures must agree.
- If a medical feature requires external hardware (such as a glucose monitor), disclose that requirement in the store listing and do not imply the app works without it. If device sensors such as a camera are used, disclose compatible device models.

## Medical functions, medical devices, and health claims

Misleading or potentially harmful health or medical functions are disallowed. If an app is not a regulated medical device, its store listing must include a clear disclaimer that it is **“not a medical device and does not diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any medical condition.”** The app must also remind users to consult a healthcare professional for medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment.

Declare regulated medical-device apps as such in the Health Apps form. Google Play may label them as medical devices and may request proof of approval, clearance, or certification from the relevant authority. Apps regulated under EU MDR 2017/745 or IVDR 2017/746 and distributed in the EEA or Northern Ireland must also publicly provide the device name; manufacturer identity, registered address, and SRN; intended-purpose description; warnings/precautions; electronic instructions for use (eIFU); and UDI-DI. Depending on the case, additional information may include an authorized representative, notified-body/certificate numbers, and operating, hardware, or security requirements.

For claims about diagnosing, monitoring, improving, or treating a condition, explain the app's purpose, claimed benefits and basis (evidence, standards, or best practices), intended users, risks, and warnings in clear language. Do not overstate diagnostic accuracy or present general wellness advice as medical diagnosis.

## Government affiliation, public health, and human-subjects research

- Health apps developed or distributed on behalf of a government or recognized healthcare organization need written authorization from that entity and must submit it through the Advance Notice process. That form accepts specified cases; it is not a general review fast track.
- Contact-tracing or health-status apps should select “Disease Prevention and Public Health” in Console and submit the required Advance Notice materials.
- Health-related human-subjects research must follow applicable rules and obtain informed consent from participants; a parent or guardian must consent for minors. It generally needs independent IRB/ethics-committee approval unless exempt. Provide approval evidence upon request. For Health Connect research, consent must explain the nature, purpose, duration, procedures, risks and benefits, confidentiality and third-party sharing, contact point, and withdrawal process. A separate research form may also be required for Health Connect data.

## Health Connect: use cases, boundaries, and data governance

Health Connect can read or write activity, body measurements, cycle tracking, nutrition, sleep, vitals, wellness/mental-health, and medical-record data. Medical records can include history, diagnoses, treatments, medications, and lab results, which are especially sensitive.

Play lists fitness/wellness, rewards, coaching, corporate wellness, medical care, health research, and games as approved use cases. Access must serve a real, clear, user-facing health feature; an approved use case cannot be extended to undisclosed secondary purposes. A health-integrated game's primary purpose must remain gaming, not an unregulated medical tool.

**Prohibited uses include:**

- Selling or transferring health/fitness data to advertising platforms, data brokers, or resellers; using it for personalized or interest-based advertising; or using it to assess creditworthiness, insurance eligibility, employment suitability, or lending.
- Sharing data with third parties without explicit, informed consent, or publicly/socially exposing sensitive health data without such consent.
- Using data for functions or recommendations not visible in the app interface, undisclosed secondary purposes, or purposes outside the approved use case.
- Reading data through headless apps; using it in apps/services solely targeting children; or using it to sync incompatible devices/platforms.
- Using it in high-risk or life-critical contexts where failure could cause death, personal injury, environmental or property damage, such as life support or air-traffic control.

Provide user help that explains how to view, manage, and delete app data, and what happens after account deactivation/deletion. Protect data with encryption in transit and at rest, strong access controls, secure development, and vulnerability management. Developers must assess whether HIPAA, GDPR, medical-device rules, and local health privacy laws apply; agreements such as a BAA or DPA may be required, and evidence may be requested by Google. Google does not warrant Health Connect data for medical, research, or diagnostic use.

## Permission requests and implementation practices

Health-related permissions are not limited to Health Connect. Play's category article lists location, activity recognition, Bluetooth, body sensors, camera, calendar, SMS, microphone, and other permissions that can collect or infer health data; it explicitly says the list is **not exhaustive**. Whether data comes from Health Connect, body sensors, or other permissions, apply these principles:

The current FAQ names these example permissions: `ACCESS_BACKGROUND_LOCATION`, `ACCESS_COARSE_LOCATION`, `ACCESS_FINE_LOCATION`, `ACTIVITY_RECOGNITION`, `BLUETOOTH_ADVERTISE`, `BLUETOOTH_CONNECT`, `BLUETOOTH_SCAN`, `BODY_SENSORS`, `BODY_SENSORS_BACKGROUND`, `CAMERA`, `READ_CALENDAR`, `READ_SMS`, `RECORD_AUDIO`, `SEND_SMS`, and `WRITE_CALENDAR`. This is not exhaustive; check the app's code, SDKs, target API, and current Android/Play rules. If a permission may access health-related sensitive data, specify the data type and purpose, then provide prominent in-app disclosure and affirmative consent. Android 16 body-sensor migration is cross-linked in [Target API requirements](../target-api/target-api-level.en-US.md) and [Sensitive Permissions](../sensitive-permissions/sensitive-permissions.en-US.md).

1. Map every permission/data type to an implemented user-facing feature, user benefit, and store description. Request only the minimum data and explain the purpose, recipient, and secondary uses in context. See the [Android Privacy Checklist](../android-privacy-checklist/android-privacy-checklist.en-US.md) for system-picker/API alternatives and disclosure examples.
2. Before requesting a permission, show prominent disclosure and affirmative consent under the [User Data policy](../user-data/user-data.en-US.md), then follow Android runtime permission flows. Stop access if the user denies or withdraws permission.
3. Give Play Console a specific reason for each data type. The FAQ's examples include `READ_MENSTRUAL_CYCLE_PHASE`, `READ_ALCOHOL_CONSUMPTION`, `READ_BLOOD_PRESSURE`, `READ_SKIN_TEMPERATURE`, and `READ_SYMPTOM_COUGH`. Symptom permissions are granular; there is no general `ALL_SYMPTOMS` permission. Reproductive health, alcohol/substance use, clinical vitals, and self-reported symptoms receive heightened scrutiny; request them only when a user-facing feature needs them.
4. Multi-purpose apps that support many data sources, especially AI agents, can use the [Matchmaking API](https://developer.android.com/health-and-fitness/health-connect/ui/matchmaking?hl=zh-CN) to check which apps/devices can provide relevant sources and avoid requesting a large set of data types up front.
5. Align Data safety, the privacy policy, in-app copy, Health Connect declaration, and actual SDK/server data flows. Provide a user-facing way to manage and delete data. If writing UDI (Unique Device Identifier) provenance metadata, keep it accurate and authentic; if reading UDI data, validate identifiers against relevant regulatory databases and assess clinical suitability yourself.
6. For Android 16 / API 36, check migration from relevant body-sensor access to granular `android.permission.health.*` permissions. Do not conflate an API migration, runtime authorization, and Play Console data-access approval.

### Matchmaking API: Narrow source discovery, not an authorization bypass

Play's Health Permissions FAQ specifically advises multi-purpose apps—especially AI agents—to learn which data sources are present before deciding whether to show permission prompts. Android's [Matchmaking API guide](https://developer.android.com/health-and-fitness/health-connect/ui/matchmaking?hl=zh-CN) implements this through a Health Connect system flow: specify record types in `MatchmakingRequest.recordTypes` and optionally include or exclude data sources; the two source filters cannot be set together. If the record-type set is empty, the flow considers all types the calling app is already permitted to read. `checkIfMatchmakingIsPossible()` can check for eligible sources before launching the system UI, where the user chooses compatible apps or devices that can write those records. The API remains experimental; check the device's `FEATURE_MATCHMAKING` status. Source matching is not Play approval for a data type and does not replace user authorization.

**Sequencing boundary still needs clarification:** Play's FAQ summary says to use Matchmaking before permission prompts, while Android's guide explicitly says the calling app must already have read authorization for the record types being matched. The API reference further explains that it matches other apps/devices that can write records the calling app may read, and the system flow can grant those sources missing write permissions. Do not interpret it as discovering arbitrary health types before first read authorization, or as bypassing Play Console declarations, review, or user consent. In practice, pass only the minimum types whose read access is already authorized. If an approval decision depends on the order of first read authorization and matching, ask Play Support to confirm the current account-specific form. The Play FAQ's short wording and the implementation guide do not fully explain the sequencing.

The same Android UI guide's [onboarding page](https://developer.android.com/health-and-fitness/health-connect/ui/onboard-users?hl=zh-CN) says Matchmaking does not support the automatic onboarding trigger that runs after permission grants. For extra education or consent steps, configure a separate exported Activity with the appropriate manifest declaration for pre-Android 14 and Android 14+ devices. Health Connect can initiate a connection directly, so the Activity may launch again (for example, after the user revokes access and reconnects); make it safe to re-enter.

[Promote Health Connect](https://developer.android.com/health-and-fitness/health-connect/ui/promote?hl=zh-CN) recommends explaining the user's benefit instead of merely describing Health Connect, using clear action labels such as “Set up,” “Get started,” or “Choose data to share,” and avoiding vague “Connect to Health Connect” wording. When requesting new data types, show only the new permissions and explain them in context. The [permissions and data access guide](https://developer.android.com/health-and-fitness/health-connect/ui/permissions?hl=zh-CN) recommends a sync toggle and a direct permission-management entry point; stop syncing when disabled, guide users to system settings after two consecutive cancellations, and explain that `revokeAllPermissions()` changes may not appear immediately without restarting the app. [Data display and attribution](https://developer.android.com/health-and-fitness/health-connect/ui/data?hl=zh-CN) recommends showing at least the source app's name (and, when available, its icon), linking to the permissions page from activity details/reports/analytics, and showing low-priority progress notifications for long-running sync. These Android UX and implementation recommendations do not replace Play requirements for purpose, minimum scope, disclosure, consent, or declaration.

The Health Connect data-type catalog groups records into activity, body measurement, cycle tracking, nutrition, sleep, vitals, and wellness, with per-type manifest permissions. The [data types guide](https://developer.android.com/health-and-fitness/health-connect/data-types) also requires developers to declare their data use and the specific types they plan to access in Play Console before publishing; permissions must first be declared in the manifest. Background reads and reads of data older than 30 days require the additional `READ_HEALTH_DATA_IN_BACKGROUND` and `READ_HEALTH_DATA_HISTORY` permissions, respectively. The guide warns that Health Connect data-type access requires special approval and users may encounter access errors if the required declaration/approval is incomplete. Its permission table applies to Health Connect SDK 1.0.0-alpha10 and later; the full field table and each type-specific API child have not been transcribed. Health Connect UI guidance emphasizes consistency, transparency about how the app works with Health Connect, and helping users reach data and permission controls from the app. The publish flow is: review policies → complete Data safety → complete the Health Apps declaration → justify each requested data type. Update the declaration when adding or dropping data types; new app submissions may trigger another review. Check current technical documentation and Play Console for actual data-type availability, SDK behavior, and approval status.

**Medical-record release status needs a separate check:** Android's [Medical Records technical guide](https://developer.android.com/health-and-fitness/health-connect/medical-records), updated October 19, 2026, says the APIs remain under development and experimental, and that Google Play's policy for Medical Records access is still being developed; apps may need additional requirements before Play Store release. On October 23, the read/write guides still described Medical Records support as Health Connect Jetpack 1.1.0-beta02 or later, while the Feature Availability guide's dependency example used 1.2.0-alpha06. Check the current version guidance for the target SDK/device; API availability, user authorization, or approval of a general Health Connect declaration does not establish Play release eligibility for medical-record reads or writes.

## Products, medicines, and health misinformation

- Apps may not facilitate the sale or purchase of prescription drugs without a prescription.
- **Core rule:** Apps may not promote or sell unapproved substances, regardless of a seller's claim that the product is legal locally. This rule appears in the current English [Health Content and Services](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511?hl=en) article and in the “Unapproved Substances” section of the consolidated [Developer Program Policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/18258653?hl=en), marked effective September 30, 2026. The English source was rechecked on October 24; its body directly links to the prohibited-products index below. The Simplified Chinese Help route remains unreadable to the browser tool.
- **Common violations (non-exhaustive):** any item on the prohibited pharmaceuticals/supplements index; products containing ephedra; hCG used for weight loss/weight control or promoted with anabolic steroids; herbal/dietary supplements with active pharmaceutical or dangerous ingredients; false or misleading claims that a product is as effective as prescription drugs or controlled substances; non-government-approved products marketed as safe/effective to prevent or treat disease; products subject to government/regulatory action or warnings; and names confusingly similar to an unapproved pharmaceutical, supplement, or controlled substance.
- The [prohibited pharmaceuticals and supplements index](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9217430?hl=en) is a dynamic, non-exhaustive example list, not the full policy or an approved-product whitelist. On October 24, it was opened from link ID 20 in the current Health Content and Services body and separately reopened from the consolidated policy (full 540-line page). It has four example headings: unapproved pharmaceuticals/supplements, weight loss, ephedra, and anabolic steroids. The page presents names as examples; the list is dynamic and non-exhaustive, so the KB does not turn it into a stable policy list or whitelist. Check the live index and applicable laws before release.
- **Practical screen:** Check ingredients, product names/aliases, listing and promotional claims, health-effect claims, and government/regulatory warnings. Review whether the app promotes, sells, or facilitates transactions for the product. Absence from the illustrative index is not evidence of approval; a local-legality claim is not an exception. Keep the separate prescription-drug-without-a-prescription rule distinct.
- Google Play's health policy recommends consulting [LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/) for monitored substances. LegitScript is a third-party information source, not Google Play policy, a whitelist, or proof of Play approval.
- The legacy standalone [Unapproved Substances policy-section URL](https://play.google.com/about/restricted-content/unapproved-substances/) remains inaccessible to this browser tool. On October 24, clicking its exact body link (ID 3) from the reopened prohibited-products index still returned Cache miss; an October 23 direct open returned Internal Error. The summary above is based on the accessible current Help and consolidated-policy bodies; it does not claim to have captured that URL or infer that the URLs redirect to one another. The standalone target body and descendants remain unknown.
- Misleading health claims that contradict medical consensus or may harm users are disallowed, including claims that vaccines alter DNA and advocacy of harmful, unapproved treatments or conversion therapy.
- Transactions for regulated clinical services should not use Google Play Billing. See the [Payments policy FAQ](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10281818?hl=en) and the [payments, billing, and subscriptions topic](../payments/payments-and-subscriptions.en-US.md). This billing exception does not waive health, privacy, or local-law requirements.

## Pre-release checklist

- [ ] Complete the Health Apps declaration; submit “no health features” when applicable.
- [ ] Check Organization account requirements, feature category, regulated-device status, and supporting credentials.
- [ ] Inventory manifest permissions, SDKs, Health Connect data types, sensors, and inferred data; map each to a user feature and benefit.
- [ ] Remove unnecessary permissions; check Android 16 health-permission migration and minimum data-type scope.
- [ ] Align the privacy policy, Data safety, listing, prominent disclosures, consent, retention/deletion, and security controls.
- [ ] Review ads/data sharing/secondary use, children-only targeting, life-critical use, research ethics, and medical claims.
- [ ] Review pharmaceuticals/supplements, health claims, prescription-drug transactions, and clinical-service billing.
- [ ] Prepare specific authorization, regulatory, or ethics records for government/health-provider affiliation, public-health, medical-device, or research functions.

This is the Android platform API reference for `android.health.connect.HealthPermissions`, described as permissions for accessing Health Connect APIs. The class is listed as added in **API level 34, U Extensions 7**. Health-claim best practices call for an understandable explanation of the app's purpose, claimed benefits and basis (evidence/standards/best practices), intended users, risks, and required warnings/disclaimers. Example disclosures tie a specific data type to a specific feature (such as activity data for estimating energy expenditure or location for showing running routes), rather than saying only that an app “needs” a permission. These are policy and declaration aids, not a substitute for medical-device review or applicable law.

The March 5, 2025 announcement requires eligible use cases for health-record access, a user-benefit justification for each data type, and tighter data-sharing, usage, and consent safeguards.
