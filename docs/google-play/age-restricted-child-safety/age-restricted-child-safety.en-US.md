## Classify the product path first

| App functionality | Main Play requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Facilitates real-money gambling, games, or contests | Use Play Console's Restrict Minor Access tool and meet the applicable real-money gambling policy/licensing requirements |
| Core functionality is dating or matchmaking | Use Restrict Minor Access to block minors |
| Core functionality randomly connects strangers for spontaneous chat | Use Restrict Minor Access and comply with applicable Child Safety Standards |
| Core functionality is anonymous communication that deliberately hides one or more users' identities | Use Restrict Minor Access and applicable Child Safety Standards; do not declare an anonymous-chat app as targeting children |
| Dating/matchmaking is only an incidental feature | Blocking the whole app may not be required, but use effective age-gating safeguards to keep minors out of that feature |
| Social, dating, anonymous-chat, or random-chat features target children | Follow Families and child-safety rules; anonymous-chat apps may not target children |

**Key distinction:** Restrict Minor Access blocks the whole app. An age gate for an incidental dating feature only limits that feature. Completing the IARC rating or selecting an 18+ target audience by itself does not mean the Play Console Restrict Minor Access control has been enabled.

## Restrict Minor Access for the whole app

- For in-scope apps, set the target age to **18 and over only** in Play Console's Target audience and content settings, then enable Restrict Minor Access.
- Google identifies minors based on Google Account age information or its systems' age determination. Users determined to be minors cannot search for, download, or purchase the app on Play.
- Minors who already installed the app may continue using it, but cannot renew existing subscriptions or make new purchases. The rule is not equivalent to disabling every existing installation.
- The age-restriction control does not replace target-audience declarations, content ratings, Families rules, child-safety obligations, or local age/consumer-protection law.

The policy uses core functionality as an important scope boundary. If dating/matchmaking is only an add-on, restrict minors from entering that feature with effective age-gating safeguards. A neutral age screen should not prefill an adult birth date or tell users which age to enter to gain access.

## Target-audience declaration and age-design guidance

The full [Manage target audience and app content settings](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9867159?hl=en) guide was reopened on October 22, 2026. New apps and every app update must declare target age groups. Select multiple groups only when the app is designed for and appropriate for each. Before submitting Target audience and content, declare ad presence, provide reviewer access instructions, and add a privacy policy. Google reviews the accuracy of the declaration; some accounts/app categories may take seven days or longer. For audiences under 21, consider whether local law treats them as children.

The age-band examples on this page are design guidance, not separate Play eligibility categories or a globally uniform legal-age chart. Ages 5 and under, 6–8, and 9–12 are considered children in most locales; 13–15 and 16–17 may be children in some locales. Any child-inclusive audience triggers Families requirements. For mixed audiences, use a neutral age screen or other appropriate controls when showing child-inappropriate ads, and use compliant ad SDKs for children and users of unknown age.

| Age group | Design fit suggested by Google | Common concerns to consider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 and under | Support non-readers/early readers; use little text, simple navigation and large touch targets; favor sensory exploration, pretend play, simple problem-solving, and a clear parent role. | Scary/dark scenes, fast reactions/fine-motor tasks, abstract memory tasks, penalties, violence or mild sexualization, and distracting feature sprawl. |
| 6–8 | Support early reading/writing; offer positive feedback, humorous stories/characters, positive role models, age-appropriate goals such as badges/unlocks, and foundational learning. | Very-young-child presentation, body stereotypes, objectifying/sexualized depictions, realistic violence/injury, and search. |
| 9–12 | Use age-appropriate logic/spatial problems, school subjects, creative expression, and personalization; keep sharing limited and supervised. | Overly childish or monotonous content, profanity, appearance stereotypes, realistic violence/injury, and unmoderated direct user interaction. |
| 13–15 | More sophisticated stories, humor, challenges, and mechanics; age-appropriate social features and material about identity/adolescence. | Glamorized violence, demeaning/highly sexualized portrayals, or risky challenges that could cause physical injury. |
| 16–17 | More complex problem-solving, school content, teen interests, appropriate social features, and positive emotional expression. | Extreme violence, sexual/domestic violence, addictive design, or high spending thresholds. |
| 18 and over | Adult-oriented content must still be lawful and policy-compliant. If the age-restriction policy requires blocking minors, enable Restrict Minor Access separately. | Selecting 18+ in the declaration alone does not enable the blocking control or replace other policies. |

Across ages, also check product quality, age-appropriate functionality and messaging, ads, and in-app purchases. Keep design recommendations separate from formal policy checks: an age declaration does not replace content ratings, Families, privacy/data, age-restriction, or ads requirements.

## Child Safety Standards

Google Play's current Child Endangerment article requires anonymous-chat, random-chat, social, and dating apps to comply with Child Safety Standards. Implement the following as real product, moderation, and response processes:

1. **Published standards:** Publicly prohibit child sexual abuse and exploitation (CSAE) in terms of service, community guidelines, or another public user-policy document.
2. **In-app feedback/reporting:** Provide an in-app mechanism for users to submit safety concerns, reports, or feedback, and accurately self-certify it in Play Console where required.
3. **CSAM response:** Take appropriate action, including removal/handling, after actual knowledge of child sexual abuse material, consistent with published standards and applicable law.
4. **Legal reporting:** Maintain a process for reporting confirmed CSAM to NCMEC or the relevant regional authority as required by applicable law.
5. **Child-safety point of contact:** Designate a contact who can receive Google Play notices, explain moderation/enforcement procedures, and drive action.

For anonymous/random chat, an age gate alone is insufficient. Also manage UGC risk, report queues, escalation and response, evidence handling, repeat offenders, and jurisdiction-specific law. CSAE/CSAM is a serious safety issue; have the responsible trust-and-safety team and counsel review operational procedures.

## Implementation guidance and child links

Google's [Learning more about Child Safety Standards](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14747720?hl=en) article was reopened on October 24, 2026. It uses two scope tests: Anonymous/Random Chat is assessed by core functionality; Social/Dating scope follows the category declared in Play Console. Whether children use the app, whether it is adult-only, or whether it has an age gate does not change applicability. The [category and tags guide](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9859673) says settings should accurately reflect the app's main function and should be revisited after significant content or functionality changes. This is store-listing guidance, not a child-safety certification. The public standards page must be globally accessible, load correctly, address CSAE/child safety, and name the app or developer as shown on Google Play. The in-app feedback mechanism must work without sending the user outside the app. CSAM response follows the developer's published process and relevant laws. Developers must self-certify in Play Console and designate a person who can explain prevention, review, and enforcement procedures.

Google Search Help's [regional reporting directory](../child-safety-regional-reporting/child-safety-regional-reporting.en-US.md) lists organizations and websites by region. The directory entry, page-access states, and its relationship to Play duties are tracked separately; readable external site bodies and their deeper links remain subject to the per-URL audit status.

Google links to the Tech Coalition's [Developer good practices for combating online child sexual exploitation and abuse](https://technologycoalition.org/resources/developer-good-practices-combating-online-child-sexual-exploitation-and-abuse/) as additional guidance. Published October 14, 2024, this is cross-industry guidance, not a new mandatory Google Play technical method, legal advice, or a universal standard of care. Its recommendations form an operational chain:

1. **Write public rules and internal procedures:** Publicly prohibit OCSEA in terms, acceptable-use policies, or community guidelines; separately document internal definitions, enforcement standards, and reviewer guidance.
2. **Enable reports and prepare detection:** Provide reporting surfaces across relevant product areas. Based on product risk and capability, consider hash matching, image/video/text classifiers, keyword and URL rules, and adequate human review capacity.
3. **Build incident response for the relevant jurisdiction:** For U.S. companies, the guide describes the NCMEC CyberTipline, Electronic Service Provider registration, reporting APIs/manual forms, and data preservation. Outside the U.S., it points to INHOPE for local hotline discovery. Reporting duties, evidence retention, and law-enforcement requests depend on applicable local law.
4. **Move safety into product design:** Assess OCSEA risks during product development, maintain law-enforcement outreach/response processes, and use safety education and suitable transparency reporting to review risks and measures over time.

## NCMEC CyberTipline: reporting routes, ESP API, and U.S. legal boundary

These are legal, technical, and victim-support context; they do not turn one API, reporting deadline, or workflow into a universal Google Play duty.

**Keep three mechanisms separate:**

1. **Google Play Child Safety Standards** require applicable developers to publish child-safety standards, offer in-app feedback, act on CSAM encountered, report as applicable law requires, complete Play Console self-certification, and name a child-safety contact. This is the Play platform-policy layer.
2. **NCMEC's public CyberTipline form** accepts reports about child sexual exploitation. NCMEC's FAQ says anyone can report regardless of U.S. residence and a reporter need not provide a name or contact details; incident and reporter locations can help route the report. NCMEC reviews reports and makes them available to law enforcement when it can identify a jurisdiction. A public reporting route is not the same as one statutory procedure for every Play developer.
3. **The CyberTipline Reporting API** is an automated route for eligible service providers. The documentation requires HTTPS and credentials supplied by NCMEC, and distinguishes production and test environments; status and XSD routes also require authentication. The workflow is `submit`, optional `upload` and `fileinfo`, then `finish`; a reporter may `retract` before completion. An unfinished draft is automatically deleted 24 hours after opening or one hour after its last modification, whichever is later. NCMEC's API documentation says that, apart from incident type and date/time, other data sent through the web service is voluntary and at an ESP's initiative. That describes API fields; do not rewrite it as a general legal conclusion that a statutory report may omit required information.

### Scope and preservation under 18 U.S.C. § 2258A

The readable [U.S. Code text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title18/html/USCODE-2024-title18-partI-chap110-sec2258A.htm) applies to a “provider” defined by § 2258E as an electronic communication service provider or remote computing service. Whether a particular service/entity meets that definition requires fact-specific legal review; do not assume every Play developer does. Section 2258A(a) requires a provider with actual knowledge of specified apparent violations to report to NCMEC's CyberTipline “as soon as reasonably possible”; it uses permissive wording for facts indicating a possible planned or imminent violation. Section 2258A(f) says this section itself does not require providers to monitor users or communications, or affirmatively search, screen, or scan for the specified facts. That does not remove obligations arising under other laws, legal process, contracts, or separate Play policy.

A completed CyberTipline report is treated under § 2258A(h) as a request to preserve the submitted contents for **one year**. The section also covers reasonably accessible commingled content that may provide context, requires secure storage and access limited to what is necessary, and requires preservation methods consistent with the latest NIST Cybersecurity Framework. The REPORT Act (Pub. L. 118-59, approved May 7, 2024) changed the former 90-day period to one year and added the NIST method provision with a one-year implementation period (May 7, 2025, now passed). The 2024 U.S. Code text and amendment notes are readable; the House preliminary Code page's search index says the law was in effect through September 13, 2026, but direct access returned HTTP 500 this pass. This identifies rules and version boundaries for follow-up; it is not a substitute for U.S. counsel reviewing the covered entity, triggers, retained materials, exceptions, or later amendments.

## OCSEA product-risk assessment, tools, and cited descendants

Tech Coalition's [assessment landing page](https://technologycoalition.org/resources/assessing-ocsea-harms-in-product-development/) was published November 2, 2023 and provides the full [14-page product-development assessment PDF](https://technologycoalition.org/wp-content/uploads/Assessing_OCSEA_Harms_in_Product_Development_FINAL.pdf). The landing page says the resource focuses on OCSEA and is voluntary material for internal discussion, not legal/regulatory advice or a standard of care. The PDF proposes an adaptable four-step process:

1. **Define the feature scenario:** Describe the specific feature, intended and actual users (including whether children may use it), content/data, relationships it enables, and likely next steps for a child and a malicious actor.
2. **Map harms and contexts:** Use the 4Cs—content, contact, conduct, and contract—to consider exposure, interaction, behavior, commercial, and overuse risks. Consider modifiers such as anonymity/ephemerality, encryption, payments, media, voice/video, location, personal information, advertising/third-party sharing, notifications, and recommendations.
3. **Estimate inherent risk:** The PDF illustrates adding likelihoods across harm types and an impact factor for the ability to detect OCSEA, then multiplying by the likelihood that a minor uses the feature. Its example is `(2+3+2+1+0)×3=24`. The authors describe scores, weights, and scales as adjustable discussion aids, advise distinguishing age groups and watching for bias, and recommend reassessment as the product changes. This number is not a Play risk rating or pass threshold.
4. **Select and validate mitigations:** Assess residual risk after policy, reporting, high-confidence proactive detection and human review, safety education, conscious-use design, or age/behavior restrictions. Continue to check effectiveness, circumvention, and changing risk.

This model can help teams connect risk analysis to product features and iteration, but it does not replace Play's public child-safety standards, in-app feedback, CSAM response, compliance with applicable law, Play Console self-certification, or point-of-contact requirements. A score is not a quantitative safety guarantee.

### Play Age Signals API: terms, sharing flow, and direct descendants

The API overview's dedicated terms (marked last modified October 2025) limit use to age-appropriate content/experiences required by law. Advertising, marketing, profiling, and analytics are prohibited. The API is limited to apps updated through Google Play, and returned information may be used only by the requesting app. Violations may terminate API access and lead to app suspension or removal. The overview says the Age Signals client library itself collects no data; developers must still assess their own app's data flows for the Data safety form. “The library collects no data” does not mean the whole app has nothing to disclose.

Implementation starts by requesting sharing status and handling it before retrieving a signal: call `checkAgeSignals()` only for `SHARED`; `NOT_SHARED` returns no age signal; `VERIFICATION_REQUIRED` means the user must resolve age verification/supervision in Play Store in an applicable region. Elsewhere, prompts follow the user's or parent's global/per-app sharing setting. Play returns an age range, not a birth date. Defaults are 0–12, 13–15, 16–17, and 18+; custom bands are configured in Console. Significant-change approval applies only to supervised users. Developers must determine legal triggers and restrict affected content/features based on approval status.

## Families and target-audience declarations

- New apps and updates must accurately complete Target audience and content. Selecting multiple age groups means the app is designed and appropriate for each selected group; do not include children only to broaden reach.
- Google may assess accuracy using the app's actual content, marketing images, and wording. The developer's self-description is not the only factor.
- A child-inclusive audience can trigger extra requirements for SDKs/APIs, data, ads, social features, content, ratings, and local child-privacy law.
- Mixed-audience apps should use a neutral age screen to separate ads/SDKs that are not appropriate for children. Ads shown to children or users of unknown age need compliant Families ad SDKs; personalized ads to children are prohibited.
- Families requirements add controls for child-facing social features, including safety reminders, adult controls, and adult action before children can exchange personal information. Anonymous-chat apps may not target children.

#### AdMob/GMA Next-Gen child age treatment (TFAT)

The Android Targeting guide, last updated October 21, 2026, uses Tag for Age Treatment (TFAT). The AdMob Families help page still shows the older `setTagForChildDirectedTreatment` example, but the TFAT Help article and current GMA Next-Gen guide explicitly say TFCD/TFUA are deprecated. Use the documentation for the actual SDK product line being integrated. This is ad-request implementation guidance; it does not replace Play target-audience declarations, Families policy, age assurance, or local-law analysis.

- Before requesting ads, set `RequestConfiguration.Builder.setAgeRestrictedTreatment(CHILD | TEEN | UNSPECIFIED)` and apply it with `MobileAds.setRequestConfiguration()`. This is global configuration and replaces the existing configuration. To apply it to the first request, provide it during SDK initialization.
- `CHILD` requests receive child age treatment: personalized ads and remarketing are disabled; requests to third-party ad-measurement pixels/ad servers are disabled; child ad-serving protections apply; and AAID/IDFA are not transmitted. `TEEN` disables personalization and remarketing and applies teen protections. `UNSPECIFIED` is the default and does not mean that user age has been determined.
- When multiple age/privacy signals apply, the most restrictive signal takes precedence. Google's tools do not remove the developer's duty to determine age treatment and comply with laws in applicable jurisdictions.
- Play Families separately requires eligible self-certified ad SDKs for child/unknown-age ad serving. Adapter versions in the table below only identify versions that forward the TFAT signal through mediation; **they do not certify the adapter or ad SDK under Play's Families Self-Certified Ads SDK Program**. #### FTC Six-Step Plan: operational content and the Play control boundary

The FTC's May 2026 [A Six-Step Compliance Plan](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule-six-step-compliance-plan-your-business) organizes COPPA work into six steps. It is practical guidance for operators within U.S. COPPA scope, not the regulation itself; determine coverage under current 16 CFR Part 312.

| FTC step | Practical summary | Relationship to Google Play |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Determine coverage | Assess whether a service is directed to children under 13 and collects or lets third parties collect their personal information; a general-audience service may also be covered when it has actual knowledge of direct child-data collection. Mobile apps, connected games, location services, ad networks, and plug-ins can be covered services or collection partners. A mixed-audience service may choose COPPA protection only for users identified as under 13, but must use a neutral age screen before collecting personal information. | COPPA coverage differs from Play target-audience declarations, Families audience categories, and age-restricted feature rules; one decision does not replace another. |
| 2. Post a compliant privacy policy | Clearly identify operators and contacts, data types and uses, recipients and disclosure purposes, retention/deletion practices, and how parents can review/delete information or refuse further collection/use; make the link easy to find. | This relates to Play privacy policies and Data safety, but Play's placement, content, and in-app availability requirements remain governed separately by User Data and Data safety. |
| 3. Give direct parental notice | Before collection, explain data types, purposes, third-party disclosures, the privacy policy, and consent method; update notice after material changes. Parents must be able to choose collection/use without non-integral third-party disclosure. | COPPA notice/email flow is not a substitute for Play's in-app prominent disclosure and affirmative-consent requirements. |
| 4. Obtain verifiable parental consent | Except for narrow Rule exceptions, obtain consent reasonably designed to verify a parent before collecting, using, or disclosing personal information. The FTC does not require one verification technology for all operators; some exceptions from prior consent can still require notice. | This legal duty is distinct from the Play age screen, parent-approval tool, and Play Console declarations; evaluate those controls separately. |
| 5. Honor continuing parental rights | Let parents review children's information, revoke consent and stop further collection/use, and request deletion. Take reasonable steps to confirm the requester is a parent without making the process unduly burdensome. | This complements Play account/data deletion and child controls; apply the Play account-deletion policy for its specific links, deadlines, and declarations. |
| 6. Secure, retain, and delete | Maintain a written security program suited to data sensitivity and the organization's size/scope; obtain written safeguards from service providers; maintain written retention/deletion procedures, minimize collection, and securely delete data when the stated purpose no longer needs it. | This overlaps with but does not replace Play User Data, Data safety, SDK responsibility, or security requirements; third-party SDK flows must also be included in Play declarations. |

## Pre-release checklist

1. Map real user flows and identify core/add-on functions involving random stranger connections, anonymous communication, dating/matchmaking, real-money participation, and UGC.
2. Declare target age, content rating, and applicable Console forms based on actual functionality; do not rely on category names alone.
3. If the app is in a category that must block minors, select only 18+ and enable Restrict Minor Access. If dating is incidental, verify the feature-level age gate also covers entry and purchase flows.
4. If Child Safety Standards apply, publish CSAE-prohibiting rules, implement in-app reporting, define CSAM response/legal-reporting procedures, and maintain a working child-safety contact.
5. For child-inclusive audiences, audit SDKs, Data safety/privacy disclosures, Families ad SDKs, age screens, social features, and local child-protection rules.
6. Reassess declarations whenever content, functionality, audience, or advertising SDKs change; retain records of reports, response, escalation, and training.
